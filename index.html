    Start here

    Start solids once your baby shows all of these signs, and run your plan by your pediatrician, especially if food allergies run in the family.

    Signs your baby is ready
    • Sits up with little or no support and holds their head steady
    • Opens their mouth and leans in when food comes near
    • No longer pushes food straight back out with their tongue
    • Shows interest in what you're eating
    First-year rules
    No honey before 12 months

    Not even baked in. It can cause infant botulism, a severe food poisoning.

    No cow's milk as a drink, and no juice

    Breast milk or formula stays the main food until 12 months.

    No added salt or sugar

    Babies don't need either, and their kidneys can't handle much salt.

    Cook meat to a safe temperature

    Ground beef 160°F, chicken and turkey 165°F, fish 145°F. Use a thermometer.

    Smooth and thin at first

    Runny purees now, thicker and lumpier over the next few months. No whole nuts, whole grapes, popcorn or hard raw vegetables.

    Vary the grains

    Rice cereal can carry arsenic. Oat, barley and mixed-grain infant cereals give iron too.

    Carrots, beets and spinach are fine now

    The nitrate warning for homemade vegetable purees is for babies under 3 months.

    What you'll need
    • A pot with a steamer basket (or a baby food maker)
    • A blender, food processor or stick blender
    • Silicone ice cube trays with lids (1-ounce cubes)
    • Freezer bags or small freezer-safe containers
    • Masking tape and a marker for labels
    • A fine strainer for skins (peas, blueberries)

    The basic method

    Almost every recipe follows these five steps. The recipe cards give the cooking time and anything different.

    1. Prep. Wash, peel and remove seeds, pits or tough skins. Cut into 1-inch pieces so they cook evenly.
    2. Steam. Put 1 to 2 inches of water in a pot, set the basket over it, cover, and cook until a fork slides through easily. Steaming keeps more vitamins than boiling. Save the water in the pot.
    3. Blend. Blend until completely smooth, adding liquid 1 tablespoon at a time: the saved steaming water, breast milk, formula or plain water.
    4. Check the texture. For a first-time eater it should drip slowly off a spoon, like thin yogurt. Over the next 2 to 3 months, add less liquid, then mash with a fork instead of blending.
    5. Cool and portion. Serve what you need and freeze the rest within 2 hours (see Storage).
    How much: start with 1 to 2 teaspoons once a day and let your baby lead. Stop when they turn away or close their mouth. Most babies work up to a few tablespoons, 2 to 3 times a day, over the next few months.
    One new food at a time: give each new food for 2 to 3 days before adding the next, so you can spot a reaction. Once a food is tolerated, mix it with others freely.

    Vegetables

    A great place to start: mild, a little sweet, and packed with vitamins.

    Combos once each is tolerated: sweet potato + apple, squash + pear, peas + zucchini, carrot + mango.

    Fruits

    Naturally sweet, so they're easy wins. Pair them with iron foods: vitamin C helps your baby absorb iron.

    Iron & protein

    The iron your baby was born with runs low around 6 months, and breast milk has little of it. Offer an iron-rich food every day.

    Introducing allergens

    Giving common allergens early, around 4 to 6 months and once your baby has handled a few other foods, can lower the chance of a food allergy. If your baby has severe eczema or an egg allergy, talk to your pediatrician first; they may want an allergy test.

    Every first taste of an allergen
    1. Pick a day your baby is healthy (no cold, vomiting or diarrhea), at home, with one adult watching closely.
    2. Offer a small taste on the tip of a spoon, then wait 10 minutes.
    3. No reaction? Give the rest of the portion slowly, at your baby's usual pace.
    4. Stay with your baby for 2 hours afterward.
    Mild reaction: a new rash or a few hives around the mouth. Stop and call your pediatrician.
    Call 911: swelling of the lips, face or tongue, vomiting, hives all over, trouble breathing, wheezing, repeated coughing, pale or blue skin, or sudden floppiness.
    Keep it up: once a food is tolerated, keep it in your baby's diet regularly, a few times a week. Never give whole nuts or spoonfuls of thick nut butter; both are choking hazards.

    Storing baby food

    Cook in batches, freeze in 1-ounce cubes, and thaw one or two at a time. Baby food keeps a much shorter time than adult leftovers.

    FoodFridgeFreezer
    Fruit and vegetable purees2 days1 month
    Meat, poultry, fish and egg purees1 day1 month
    Thawed food (any)1 dayDon't refreeze
    Out at room temperature (1 hour if over 90°F); leftovers in baby's bowl get tossed2 hours maxToss it

    Some blogs say frozen purees last 3 months or more. This guide uses the 1-month limit from FDA and university food-safety guidance: safer, and the flavor is better too.

    The ice cube tray method
    1. Cool the puree quickly: spread it in a shallow dish or set the bowl in ice water. Get it into the freezer within 2 hours of cooking.
    2. Spoon it into a clean ice cube tray. One standard cube holds about 1 ounce (2 tablespoons), a good first-meal portion.
    3. Cover with the lid, foil or plastic wrap, and freeze flat until solid, usually overnight.
    4. Pop the cubes into a freezer bag or container, squeeze out the air, and keep one food per bag.
    5. Label every bag with the food and the date it was made.
      Sweet potato · made Nov 9 · use by Dec 9

    No trays? Drop 1 to 2 tablespoon mounds onto a lined cookie sheet, freeze, then bag them the same way.

    Thawing and reheating
    Thaw in the fridge overnight

    Or warm the frozen cubes straight from the freezer on the stove or in the microwave.

    Never thaw on the counter

    Or in standing water. Bacteria grow fast at room temperature.

    Reheat until steaming, then cool

    Heat to 165°F (steaming all the way through), stir well, and let it cool. Microwaves leave hot spots, so stir and test a bit on your wrist before serving.

    Never refreeze thawed food

    Use thawed cubes within a day.

    Serving
    • Spoon a portion into a bowl. Don't feed straight from the storage container or jar: saliva on the spoon spoils what's left.
    • Throw out whatever is left in the bowl after the meal.
    • Never chew food before giving it to your baby.
    • Wash trays, containers and blender parts in hot, soapy water (or the dishwasher) between batches.
    Not medical advice. Ask your pediatrician about your baby's needs, especially with allergies, eczema, reflux or a premature birth.
