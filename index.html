          Start here

          Start solids once your baby shows all of these signs, and run your plan by your pediatrician, especially if food allergies run in the family.

          Signs your baby is ready
          • Sits up with little or no support and holds their head steady
          • Opens their mouth and leans in when food comes near
          • No longer pushes food straight back out with their tongue
          • Shows interest in what you're eating
          First-year rules
          No honey before 12 months

          Not even baked in. It can cause infant botulism, a severe food poisoning.

          No cow's milk as a drink, and no juice

          Breast milk or formula stays the main food until 12 months. Yogurt and cheese are fine.

          No added salt or sugar

          Babies don't need either, and their kidneys can't handle much salt.

          Cook meat to a safe temperature

          Ground beef 160°F, chicken and turkey 165°F, fish 145°F. Use a thermometer.

          Vary the grains

          Rice cereal can carry arsenic. Oat, barley and mixed-grain infant cereals give iron too.

          Carrots, beets and spinach are fine now

          The nitrate warning for homemade vegetable purees is for babies under 3 months.

          What you'll need
          • A pot with a steamer basket (or a baby food maker)
          • A blender, food processor or stick blender
          • Silicone ice cube trays with lids (1-ounce cubes)
          • Freezer bags or small freezer-safe containers
          • Masking tape and a marker for labels

          The basic method

          Almost every puree follows these five steps. The recipe cards give the cooking time and anything different.

          1. Prep. Wash, peel and remove seeds, pits or tough skins. Cut into 1-inch pieces so they cook evenly.
          2. Steam. Put 1 to 2 inches of water in a pot, set the basket over it, cover, and cook until a fork slides through easily. Save the water in the pot.
          3. Blend. Blend until smooth, adding liquid 1 tablespoon at a time: the saved steaming water, breast milk, formula or plain water.
          4. Match her stage. See Texture stages below: runny at first, thicker and lumpier as she grows.
          5. Cool and portion. Serve what you need and freeze the rest within 2 hours. Log it on the Freezer tab.
          How much: start with 1 to 2 teaspoons once a day and let her lead. Stop when she turns away or closes her mouth. Most babies work up to a few tablespoons, 2 to 3 times a day, over the next few months.
          One new food at a time: give each new food for 2 to 3 days before adding the next, so you can spot a reaction. Once a food is tolerated, mix it with others freely.

          Texture stages

          Moving through textures on time helps her learn to chew and keeps mealtimes interesting. Ages are a guide; follow her signs.

          Introducing allergens

          Giving common allergens early, around 4 to 6 months and once she has handled a few other foods, can lower the chance of a food allergy. With severe eczema or an egg allergy, talk to your pediatrician first.

          Every first taste of an allergen
          1. Pick a day she's healthy (no cold, vomiting or diarrhea), at home, with one adult watching closely.
          2. Offer a small taste on the tip of a spoon, then wait 10 minutes.
          3. No reaction? Give the rest of the portion slowly, at her usual pace.
          4. Stay with her for 2 hours afterward.
          Mild reaction: a new rash or a few hives around the mouth. Stop and call your pediatrician.
          Call 911: swelling of the lips, face or tongue, vomiting, hives all over, trouble breathing, wheezing, repeated coughing, pale or blue skin, or sudden floppiness.
          Keep it up: once an allergen is tolerated, keep it in her diet a few times a week. What's next reminds you when one hasn't come up in a week.

          Choking safety

          Gagging is normal while she learns; it's noisy and she works it out herself. Choking is silent. Learn infant CPR and keep these rules.

          • Always seated upright, always watched. No eating in the car seat or while crawling.
          • Finger foods should squish between your thumb and finger and be about the size of an adult finger.
          • Quarter grapes and cherry tomatoes lengthwise; flatten blueberries.
          • No whole nuts, popcorn, hard raw vegetables or apple chunks, hot dog coins, chunks of meat or spoonfuls of thick nut butter.

          Storing baby food

          Cook in batches, freeze in 1-ounce cubes, and thaw one or two at a time. Baby food keeps a much shorter time than adult leftovers.

          The ice cube tray method
          1. Cool the puree quickly (a shallow dish, or the bowl set in ice water) and get it in the freezer within 2 hours.
          2. Spoon it into a clean ice cube tray. One cube holds about 1 ounce (2 tablespoons).
          3. Cover and freeze flat until solid, usually overnight.
          4. Pop the cubes into a freezer bag, squeeze out the air, one food per bag.
          5. Label it with the food and date, and add it on the Freezer tab.

          Thawing and serving
          Thaw in the fridge overnight

          Or warm frozen cubes straight from the freezer on the stove or in the microwave. Never on the counter or in standing water.

          Reheat until steaming, then cool

          Heat to 165°F all the way through, stir well, let it cool, and test a bit on your wrist.

          Never refreeze thawed food

          Use thawed cubes within a day.

          Don't feed from the storage container

          Spoon a portion into a bowl and toss whatever's left in the bowl after the meal.
